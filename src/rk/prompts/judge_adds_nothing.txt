You are checking, facet by facet, whether a research proposal changes or adds to a paper's research idea.

The proposal deliberately uses different words, and it was written as a plan before the work was done, so its claim is an aim or expectation. Judge meaning, not wording: a method described without its name is the same method. Names of datasets, benchmarks, models, methods, platforms and organisations, and numbers, are removed on purpose: their absence never makes a facet "no", and a described stand-in (for example "a video site where comments scroll over the picture" for a named platform) counts as specific.

Step 1. Describe four facets of the paper and of the proposal, each in at most 15 words:
- problem: the research problem or question
- method: the method or approach
- domain: the domain or population studied, including any language, region, data source or user group
- claim: the main claimed contribution or finding

Step 2. For each facet, is everything the proposal says about it supported by the paper? Answer "no" if the proposal changes the facet, adds something the paper does not support (a different or extra method, domain, population, language, region, data or result, or a stronger or broader claim), or states it so generally that it no longer tells this work apart from close sibling work, for example because it drops the specific domain, population, data type or language.
Example of a too-general proposal: for a paper on recognising named entities in Kazakh with labels generated by a language model, "We will improve a language technology using automatically created training data" fits many papers, so its domain and method facets are "no".

Step 3. The verdict is "yes" only if all four facets are "yes".
